## Findings
1. In a multinormal model of winner-take-all contests, raising one's variability and lowering one's correlation with rivals improves the odds of winning whenever the winners are fewer than half the contestants, and the advice reverses when they are more than half [1].
2. In a winner-take-all forecasting contest with a common prior, honest forecasting is not an equilibrium: the unique symmetric linear equilibrium puts more weight on the private signal than the Bayesian forecast does, because rivals clustered around the prior mean make it profitable to move away from it [2].
3. When private signals carry little information, the contest equilibrium spreads forecast locations over the common prior (a Hotelling location game) instead of piling them at its mean [2].
4. Selecting the top scorer under a proper scoring rule is not incentive compatible: a forecaster just below a cluster of rivals wins only on her minority outcome (probability 0.2 in the paper's example) but on her majority outcome (0.8) by reporting beyond the cluster, and no deterministic mechanism is strictly incentive compatible [3].
5. A Kaggle March Madness winner modelled the probabilities, then competitors' likely submissions, and then searched for the entry with the highest chance of a top-five finish rather than the most accurate entry [3].
6. Forecasters who care about rank rationally report more extreme probabilities than they believe [4], and in a public-private signal model the resulting exaggeration of private information makes the competitive crowd's average more accurate than a truthful crowd's [5].
7. In simulated 300-contestant, 100-question tournaments, the most accurate forecasters make nearly identical forecasts and score "as if there were just one such contestant", so the winner is usually a good-but-not-best forecaster whose errors fell toward the realised outcomes [6].
8. Across 120 Kaggle competitions, public and private scores track each other with little adaptive overfitting; the large gaps came from non-i.i.d. splits or very small test sets, and the authors name distribution shift as the more pressing problem [7].
9. On new test sets built by the original process, every ImageNet model lost 11-14 points of accuracy, but the models' relative order was largely preserved [8].
10. Field evidence agrees that trailing competitors take more risk (golf match play: more risk the further behind), though the tournament literature is not unanimous, and bold play is provably optimal for reaching a fixed goal only in subfair games whose odds do not depend on the player's position [11][12][13].

### B. One board, two uploads: the second upload is a call option on the difference
Clark [9]: let θ = sqrt(σ1² + σ2² − 2ρσ1σ2) and α = (μ1 − μ2)/θ. Then
- E[max] = μ1·Φ(α) + μ2·Φ(−α) + θ·φ(α);
- E[max²] = (μ1² + σ1²)Φ(α) + (μ2² + σ2²)Φ(−α) + (μ1 + μ2)·θ·φ(α).

The value of s2 is V2 = E[max] − μ1 = E[(X2 − X1)+] = θ·φ(δ/θ) − δ·Φ(−δ/θ), with δ = μ1 − μ2.

With equal marginals, E[max] − μ = σ·sqrt((1 − ρ)/π). At ρ = −0.5, 0, 0.5, 0.8 and 0.95 this is 0.69, 0.56, 0.40, 0.25 and 0.13σ.

Tail: P(max ≥ c) = 1 − Φ2(z1, z2; ρ). At z = 1.5 a single upload has 6.7%. The better of two has 13.0% at ρ = 0, 11.5% at 0.5, 9.9% at 0.8 and 8.3% at 0.95. At z = 2 the single upload has 2.3%, and the better of two has 4.5% (ρ = 0) or 2.9% (ρ = 0.95) (`calc_e/c1.py`).

Consequence: s2's worth depends on SD(X2 − X1) far more than on E[X2]. A low-correlation backup can afford a lower mean:
- δ = 0.5σ, ρ = 0: worth 0.35σ;
- a local variant with +0.2σ of mean and ρ = 0.95: worth 0.25σ;
- δ = 1σ, ρ = 0.2: worth only 0.16σ.

So contrarianism has a price ceiling. Our T1 and heart pairs are already straddles: the second upload adds +4.7 on each in reading A against B. This is the structure the theory recommends.

### C. The additive Overall: where a unit of variance pays
Write P(O ≥ c) ≈ Φ((μ − c)/σ), with z = (c − μ)/σ > 0 when trailing. Then
- ∂P/∂μ_k = w_k·φ(z)/σ;
- ∂P/∂Var(B_k) = w_k²·φ(z)(c − μ)/(2σ³);
- ∂P/∂Cov(B_k, B_l) = w_k·w_l·φ(z)(c − μ)/σ³.

1. Per unit of board variance, T1 and T3 are worth four times a T2 board. Per unit of board SD (∂σ/∂s_k = w_k²·s_k/σ), variance is worth most where it already sits, which is T3. This is the concentration principle behind bold play [13].
2. The laggard's certainty equivalent is CE = μ + κσ², with κ = (c − μ)/(2σ²). A change helps iff ΔE[O] + κ·ΔVar(O) > 0. From reading A, κ is 0.020, 0.038, 0.056 and 0.074 Overall points per unit of Var(O) at c = 196, 200, 204 and 208. The board exchange rate is w_k·κ board-mean points per unit of that board's variance (`calc_e/c4.py`).
   - Worked case: at c = 204, a T1 s2 change that costs 1 point of E[max] must add about 18 to Var(O) (T1's SD from 3.2 to 5.3) to break even.
   - That is why D3 (−0.39 mean) lost at 8 of 9 levels in `options_tail`.
3. ∂P/∂σ = φ(z)·z/σ peaks at z = 1. For c from 200 to 208 our z is 0.8 to 1.6, so variance is near its highest marginal value now.
4. Where a board's outcome matters: g_k(x) = Φ((x − τ_k)/s_k), with τ_k = (c − E[O₋k])/w_k and s_k = SD(O₋k)/w_k. At c = 200 / 204 / 208:

   | Board | τ_k | s_k | Character |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | T3 | 73 / 77 / 81 | ≈ 4.7 | jackpot: only outcomes of about 70 or more count |
   | T1 | 78 / 82 / 86 | ≈ 10 | broad and convex |
   | Heart | 71 / 79 / 87 | ≈ 21 | almost linear |
   | Embryo | 78 / 86 / 94 | ≈ 21 | almost linear |

   - The top-3 value of a candidate s2_j on board k is V_kj = E[g_k(max(X_k1, X_kj))] − E[g_k(X_k1)].
   - On T2, only the mean matters. On T3, only the upper tail matters.
5. A covariance between the two weight-1 boards is worth twice a T1-heart covariance and four times a heart-embryo one.

### D. Rank, not level: the mimicry discount λ (new; not in `options_tail`/`portfolio_tail`)
The top-3 line is the third-best total in the field. That total moves with the hidden targets, as ours does. Model it as C3 = c0 + λ·Σ_k w_k(X_k1 − E X_k1) + η. Our consensus-like first uploads stand in for the field's common shock, and η is the third place's own noise.

Then O − C3 = Σ_k w_k[(1 − λ)X_k1 + λ·E X_k1 + D_k+] − c0 − η, where D_k = X_k2 − X_k1.
- At λ = 1 (our s1 fully mimetic), only the second upload's gain over the first (D_k+) creates rank variance. At λ = 0 we are back to the absolute model, which is the only case `options_tail` and `portfolio_tail` priced.
- The rank-aware certainty equivalent uses κ_λ = (c0 − μ)/(2·Var(O − λ·S1)). Since Var(O − λ·S1) is much smaller than Var(O) as λ → 1, κ_λ is several times κ, and backups that are uncorrelated with s1 and bold gain value.

Synthetic check (`calc_e/c3.py`, stylised on reading A):

| c0 | λ | today-like | bold T3 s2 (q = 0.25) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 204 | 0 | 7.6% | 8.6% |
| 204 | 0.5 | 1.9% | 4.3% |
| 204 | 1 | 3.4% | 8.2% |
| 200 | 1 | 6.8% | 13.1% |

- Today-like means a T1 straddle with a near-WT T3 s2. The bold T3 s2 reaches about 70 with q = 0.25 when s1 fails.
- A high-correlation T1 variant with a higher mean wins at λ = 0, and its edge shrinks as λ rises.
- Reading: if the field's third place shares our s1's fortunes, our chance rests on how big and how uncorrelated the s2 gains are.
- λ cannot be read without competitor material. It is the captain's judgement: high where our s1 follows the baseline-anchored consensus (section F), lower where it carries our own information (chamber scope, the T3 decomposition).

### E. When to shade away from the consensus
Shade the backup further when:
- (C1) the prize goes to a small share of the field [1];
- (C2) we trail, with z > 0;
- (C3) the board weighs 1;
- (C4) rivals are anchored on a public prior, here the published baselines and the organisers' hints [2];
- (C5) our private information is informative, since the contest weight on the signal C exceeds the Bayesian weight t/(n + t) [2];
- (C6) the downside is protected, as it is by the better of two.

Do not shade when:
- the payoff is concave: the T2 structure gate multiplies the shape credit by min(1, 2·skill(neighbourhoods));
- the field's spread is narrow: on the embryo board "everyone can do reasonably well" (`docs/PLAN.md` section 1);
- the signal is pure noise: drawn noise is rank variance, but its option value is only about 0.56 × the draw SD.

Leapfrog rule [3]: if the field clusters at a value and our belief favours one side, the backup goes clearly beyond the cluster on that side. A backup inside the cluster only splits the prize. Witkowski's numbers are a winning chance of 0.2 when reporting inside the cluster and 0.8 when reporting beyond it.

Scorer-specific result (toy, `calc_e/c5.py`). The skill map divides each error by the floor's own error, x = |pred − truth|/|floor − truth|, so for a large true change the good region is wide and for a small one it is narrow. The toy puts the floor at 0 and the truth uniform on [0.3, 0.7] along one axis.
- A single upload's E[skill] peaks at 0.45-0.50, a mild conservatism premium.
- P(skill ≥ 0.9) keeps rising up to 0.6.
- With s1 at 0.45, the best second upload sits at about 0.6 (E[max] 0.896), not at 0.3 (0.864).

So the backup should exaggerate the change away from the floor, not hedge toward it. This holds for the distance metrics (mmd_u, variogram, the T2 shape metrics). It does not hold for de_score or de_direction, which are rank-based and ignore magnitude.

T3: under the working answer (a wrong sign scores skill 0.5, confidence 0.65), |severity_slope| is a free call on getting the sign right, while de_score and de_direction can fall below 0.5. Under the "undefined → 0" reading the free call is gone.

### G. Cross-board coherence
For a laggard, a positive correlation between board outcomes raises P(O ≥ c) at no cost to E[O] (C.5). Both the T1 backup (CM-rich) and the heart backup (heart-focused half trim) win if the E12.5 samples are more cardiomyocyte- and chamber-dominated than E10.5. ### H. Sequence, information and final marks
- Scores publish at upload: T1, heart and embryo s1 go first, and T3's s2 goes first. The gate already places our first round late (2026-11-27).
- A switch between pre-built backups keyed to our round-1 outcome is "scores choose" under the official rule. But it conflicts with two of our own rules: `docs/S2_RULES.md` ("No rule reads a test score") and the DATA_POLICY ban on leaderboard figures as thresholds. Lever 5 covers it.

### I. Shake-ups and overfitting to validation
- The test board has no public/private split, but validation → test is a non-i.i.d. shift: a new stage, a two-day horizon and a new knockout. That is the case where [7] found large gaps.
- Against that, [8] found ranks preserved under a milder shift.
- The P2 board allowed eight scored submissions a day, so its leaders are partly selected on noise (digest 2.7). With two blind uploads, close methods will be ranked by small idiosyncratic differences ([6]; [7] section 5).
- For us: the third place's own noise η helps a trailing team slightly. Our defence against validation overfitting, the adoption rule on held-out backtests, is the right one.

### J. Conflicting objectives
The six prize places and the Generality Award (final standing among eligible entries) reward E[O] more than the top-3 line does. Rule [1] still says to add variance for any k far below N/2, but less for k = 6 than for k = 3. Proposed guard (the captain's call): accept a variance-seeking change only if it costs at most 0.5 points of E[O].

### K. Decision rules, usable with the committed arrays (aggregation only, not a try)
1. Laggard screen: ΔE[O] + κ(c)·ΔVar(O) > 0, with κ = (c − E[O])/(2·Var(O)).
2. Per-board value of a backup: V_kj (C.4), averaged over the captain's c-prior.
3. Rank-aware pricing:
   - Per draw i, take S1_i = Σ w_k·x_k1[i], O_i = Σ w_k·max(x_k1[i], x_k2[i]), and C3_i = c0 + λ(S1_i − mean S1) + η_i, with η_i ~ N(0, 2-4²).
   - P_top3 = mean(O_i ≥ C3_i).
   - Report it for λ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}, with c0 over our 75th-99th percentiles. On T3 the λ term uses s1's stand-ins.
4. Recommend a backup change only if it raises P_top3 at λ = 0.5 and λ = 1 across the c0 range and passes the guard in J.

## Candidate levers

### 1. Rank-aware pricing of every second upload (the mimicry discount λ)

- **Effort:** 0.5 day.

## Deep Read Notes
- [2] Ottaviani & Sorensen:
  - A winner-take-all contest is Hotelling location with private signals. Honest forecasting is not an equilibrium (Proposition 4).
  - The unique linear equilibrium weight C = (sqrt(t² + 4nt) − t)/2n exceeds the honest t/(n + t), where n is the prior precision (Proposition 5).
  - "The tendency of opponents to cluster around the prior mean drives forecasters away from it." With uninformative signals, locations replicate the prior.
  - The result survives small common errors (Proposition 8) and extends to multiple dimensions (section 7.2). Late forecasters overshoot the consensus.
- [3] Witkowski et al.:
  - The top-score rule is not incentive compatible: in the 0.8/0.9 example, reporting beyond the cluster lifts the winning chance from 0.2 to 0.8 (Appendix C).
  - No deterministic mechanism is strictly incentive compatible (Theorem 1).
  - ELF draws a lottery with f_i = 1/n + (1/n)(R_i − mean of the others' R_j).
  - The Kaggle March Madness winner optimised P(top 5) using a model of rivals' submissions.
- [6] Aldous:
  - With 300 contestants and RMS errors spread over [0, 0.3], the winner is most often around the 100th most accurate. Near-perfect forecasters score alike, as one ticket.
  - The paradox weakens when all contestants are inaccurate.
  - Real errors are correlated across contestants, which the model ignores. That is the mimetic case where deviating in the right direction wins.
- [7] Roelofs et al.:
  - 120 competitions show near-diagonal public/private scores; the top-10% deviations are about 1% or less.
  - The outliers had non-i.i.d. splits or test sets of 90-209 items.
  - Rank shake-ups can arise from pure noise when many entries have near-equal scores.
  - Distribution shift is named as the bigger danger.
